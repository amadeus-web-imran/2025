*   **The Noldor:** The Noldor are **not driven out**; they choose to leave. The Valar do not hinder them (at first). Manwë specifically says they are free to go, though he warns them of the consequences.
*   **Inference:** For Tolkien, the most tragic "fall" is not the one where you are pushed, but the one where you walk away under your own power. It emphasizes **Free Will** as a terrifying responsibility. The Noldor choose "freedom" in the wild over "servitude" in paradise, making their fall a self-inflicted wound.

### 2\. The Nature of the "Forbidden Fruit"

*   **Genesis:** The sin is the consumption of the fruit from the Tree of Knowledge. It is an act of seeking parity with God through a specific, forbidden action.
*   **The Noldor:** The sin is **Sub-creation and Possession**. Fëanor’s "fire" is his creative genius. The "fruit" is the Silmarils themselves. The fall happens because he loves the work of his own hands more than the light of Eru/the Valar.
*   **Inference:** Tolkien suggests that the greatest temptation for the gifted is "Primary Pride"—believing that what we create is ours alone. This reflects Tolkien’s own anxiety as an author: the danger of an artist becoming "possessive" of their sub-created world.

### 3\. The "Fire Greater Than Thou Knowest" (The Tragic Hero)

*   **Genesis:** Adam and Eve are often portrayed as naive or easily led by the serpent. Their fall is a loss of innocence.
*   **The Noldor:** Fëanor is the most brilliant and powerful of all Elves. His fall is not a loss of innocence, but a **perversion of greatness**. He is a Miltonic figure (like Lucifer) who would rather rule in the darkness of Middle-earth than serve in the light of Valon.
*   **Inference:** Petty notes that Tolkien’s version allows for a "Tragic Hero" archetype that Genesis lacks. Fëanor is magnetic and inspiring. This teaches that evil is not just "badness," but "greatness gone wrong." The "fire" is divine, but the direction is demonic.

### 4\. The Curse vs. The Prophecy (The Doom of Mandos)

*   **Genesis:** The ground is cursed; labor becomes painful; death enters the world. These are direct changes to the laws of nature.
*   **The Noldor:** The Doom of Mandos is a **prophecy of consequence**. Mandos doesn't say "I will make you fail"; he says "You _will_ fail because of the path you have chosen." He foretells that their own works will betray them.
*   **Inference:** This suggests a "psychological" fall. The Noldor carry their own hell with them. Their "grace" isn't taken by a judge; it is burned away by their own internal "fire" and the blood they shed (the Kinslaying).

### Summary of Inferences

Through Petty's lens, we can infer that Tolkien viewed "The Fall" as a recurring cycle rather than a single historical event.

By making the Noldor’s exodus a **voluntary** act of rebellion led by a **creative** genius, Tolkien shifts the focus from **moral obedience** (Genesis) to **spiritual alignment**. He suggests that the "fire" in us is a gift from the Creator (Eru), but when that fire is used to "start again" by cutting ourselves off from the source of grace, we inevitably create a world of "shadow and mourning."
